Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning · Chapter 18 · §18.4 – §18.5.4

Make the trees disagree, or make each one fix the last one's mistakes.

Bagging averages trees grown on resampled data, but if every tree asks the same first question, the trees stay correlated and averaging stops helping. A random forest (§18.4) also hides most of the features at every split, which forces the trees apart.

Boosting (§18.5) goes the other way. It grows the trees one at a time, and each new one is fit to what the ensemble so far gets wrong. Forward stagewise additive modeling (18.5.1) is the general recipe. With squared loss, each new tree is just fit to the residuals (18.5.2). With exponential loss you get AdaBoost (18.5.3), and with log loss you get LogitBoost (18.5.4).

§18.4

Random forests: hide most features at every split

Bagging fits each tree to a bootstrap sample and averages them. That only helps if the trees are different enough. A random forest [Bre01] adds a second source of randomness: at each node \(i\), the split search of Eq. 18.5 may only look at a random subset \(S_i\subset\{1,\dots,D\}\) of the features,

\[ (j_i,t_i)=\arg\min_{j\in S_i}\ \min_{t\in\mathcal T_j}\ \frac{|\mathcal D_i^L|}{|\mathcal D_i|}c(\mathcal D_i^L)+\frac{|\mathcal D_i^R|}{|\mathcal D_i|}c(\mathcal D_i^R),\qquad |S_i|=m. \]

With \(m=D\) this is plain bagging. Common defaults are \(m\approx\sqrt D\) for classification and \(m\approx D/3\) for regression. The book's example is the HP spam data: 4601 emails, 57 features (48 word frequencies, 6 character frequencies, and 3 run-length features CAPAVE, CAPMAX, CAPTOT). On it, random forests clearly beat bagged trees (Fig. 18.5).

Explain itAt your level

If everyone in a guessing game peeks at the same clue, they all make the same mistake.

Imagine 100 kids guessing if a letter is junk mail. One clue is super obvious, like a dollar sign. If every kid looks at the dollar sign first, they all think the same way, and asking 100 kids is no better than asking one.

So you play a trick: each time a kid wants to ask a question, you hand them only a few clue cards picked at random. Sometimes the dollar sign isn't in their hand, so they have to use other clues, like "does it say FREE?" Now the kids think in different ways. Each kid is a little worse on their own, but when they vote, their mistakes cancel out and the group gets better.

Averaging only cancels out errors that are different. Two trees trained on bootstrap samples of the same data often look almost identical, because the best first split is the same in nearly every sample. Their mistakes line up, so the average is not much better than one tree.

A random forest says: at every split, pick \(m\) features at random (say 5 out of 30) and only search those. The strongest feature now gets skipped at many splits, and the trees use other features instead. Each tree gets a bit worse, but the trees become much less alike, and the vote improves.

In the Play box, watch the "root feature" chart: bagged trees almost always split on the same feature first, and random-forest trees spread their first splits out.

From §18.2, an average of \(M\) identically distributed predictors with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\) has variance \(\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\sigma^2\). More trees kill the second term only. Bagging leaves \(\rho\) high, so the \(\rho\sigma^2\) floor dominates.

Random feature subsets at every node lower \(\rho\) at the cost of a slightly higher \(\sigma^2\) and bias per tree. Smaller \(m\) means lower \(\rho\) but weaker trees, so the best \(m\) is somewhere in the middle. Sweep it in the Play box.

The trees are grown deep and unpruned (low bias, high variance), since averaging takes care of variance. Each tree is fit independently, so forests train in parallel. Boosting, which beats them on spam, has to fit its trees one after another.

  • Out-of-bag error. Each bootstrap sample leaves out about \(e^{-1}\approx 37\%\) of the points. Predicting each point with only the trees that didn't see it gives a nearly free estimate of test error, so \(m\) can be tuned without a validation split.
  • When does it help? The gain is largest when a few strong features would otherwise dominate every tree. With many irrelevant features, a very small \(m\) can hurt: most subsets contain no useful feature and the split is noise. The probability that a subset of size \(m\) misses all \(k\) relevant features is \(\binom{D-k}{m}/\binom Dm\).
  • Consistency. Breiman's forests are hard to analyze. Simplified variants (purely random forests, honest trees with separate split and estimate samples) are consistent, and Wager & Athey derive asymptotic normality for causal forests.
  • In practice. scikit-learn's max_features is \(m\) (default "sqrt" for classifiers, 1.0, i.e. bagging, for regressors since v1.1). Extremely randomized trees go further and also draw the threshold at random.

Math, slowlyWhy decorrelating helps

  1. Let each tree's prediction at \(\mathbf x\) have variance \(\sigma^2\) and let any two trees have correlation \(\rho\). The forest average \(\bar f=\frac1M\sum_m F_m\) has \[ \operatorname{Var}[\bar f]=\frac1{M^2}\Big(M\sigma^2+M(M-1)\rho\sigma^2\Big)=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\sigma^2 . \] As \(M\to\infty\) the second term vanishes and \(\rho\sigma^2\) remains. Adding trees can't lower it; only lowering \(\rho\) can.
  2. Bagging's only randomness is the bootstrap. If one feature is much stronger than the rest, nearly every bootstrap sample picks it at the root, the top of every tree is the same, and \(\rho\) is large.
  3. The forest restricts node \(i\) to \(S_i\), \(|S_i|=m\), drawn fresh at every node. The chance that the strongest feature is even available at a given node is \[ P(j^*\in S_i)=\frac{m}{D}. \] With \(D=30,m=5\), the root of only one tree in six can use the dominant feature. The other five must start elsewhere, so trees differ from the top down.
  4. The price: each tree is a little worse (higher \(\sigma^2\), some bias), because it sometimes splits on a weak or irrelevant feature. The chance that a node sees none of the \(k\) useful features is \[ P(S_i\cap\mathcal R=\varnothing)=\binom{D-k}{m}\Big/\binom{D}{m}. \] In the demo \(D=30,k=11\): 0.63 for \(m=1\), 0.39 for \(m=2\), 0.08 for \(m=5\). Small \(m\) wastes many splits on noise features; that's the left side of the sweep.
  5. Both methods fit their trees independently, so they parallelize perfectly. Boosting (next) fits tree \(m\) to the errors of trees \(1,\dots,m-1\), so it is sequential.

§18.5

Boosting: an additive model fit one piece at a time

Bagged trees and forests are already a sum of functions,

\[ f(\mathbf x;\theta)=\sum_{m=1}^M\beta_mF_m(\mathbf x;\theta_m), \tag{18.15} \]

with \(\beta_m=1/M\). Let the \(F_m\) be any function approximators and fit everything to minimize the empirical loss \(\mathcal L(f)=\sum_{i=1}^N\ell(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i))\) (18.16), and you have an additive model: a linear model with adaptive basis functions.

Boosting fits the pieces in sequence. Fit \(F_1\) to the data, then reweight the data so the examples \(F_1\) got wrong count more, fit \(F_2\), and so on up to \(M\) (chosen on a validation set, with early stopping). If each \(F_m\) is a weak learner, only a bit better than chance even on the reweighted data, the combination is a strong learner. Boosting mainly reduces bias, while bagging and forests reduce variance.

Explain itAt your level

A team where each new player practices only the shots the team keeps missing.

The first player tries to guess the answers and gets some wrong. The coach circles the ones they missed in red. The second player studies mostly the circled ones. Then the coach circles what the team still gets wrong, and the third player studies those. And so on.

No player is great alone. Each one is only a little better than flipping a coin. But because each one covers the holes the others left, the whole team gets really good.

Bagging is different: everyone practices on their own, on slightly different worksheets, and then they vote. That helps when players are good but jumpy. Boosting helps when players are too simple to get it right alone.

Think of the model as a sum of simple pieces, like stacking LEGO bricks to match a curved shape. One flat brick (a "stump", a tree with a single question) is a terrible fit. Boosting adds bricks one at a time, each placed where the current stack is furthest from the target.

Bagging also adds up simple pieces, but each piece tries to match the whole target by itself. Averaging many identical-looking stumps still gives roughly one step: the average can't be curvier than its pieces. That's bias, and voting doesn't fix it.

In the Play box, compare 100 bagged stumps with 100 boosted stumps. Then switch to full-depth trees and watch the opposite happen.

Additive model: \(f=\sum_m\beta_mF_m\) minimizing \(\sum_i\ell(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i))\). Fitting all \((\beta_m,\theta_m)\) jointly is hard, so boosting fits them greedily one at a time (§18.5.1).

Bias vs variance. Bagging averages i.i.d. fits, so \(\mathbb E[\bar f]=\mathbb E[F_m]\): the bias of one tree, with less variance. Boosted terms depend on each other: \(F_m\) is fit to what \(f_{m-1}\) misses, so the sum can represent functions no single weak learner can, which lowers bias. Each step adds capacity, so \(M\) needs to be tuned.

The original boosting [Sch90; FS96] used binary classifiers \(F_m\in\{-1,+1\}\) and came out of PAC learning theory. The statistical view [FHT00; Fri01] works with any differentiable loss: regression, multiclass, ranking.

  • Weak-to-strong. Schapire's question: is weak PAC learnability (accuracy \(\tfrac12+\gamma\) under every distribution) the same as strong? AdaBoost answers yes constructively: training error \(\le\prod_m2\sqrt{\epsilon_m(1-\epsilon_m)}\le e^{-2\sum_m\gamma_m^2}\).
  • Basis-function view. With stumps, \(f\) is a GAM: a sum of univariate step functions. Depth-\(d\) trees allow interactions up to order \(d\), so the base learner's depth sets the interaction order.
  • Regularization knobs beyond \(M\): shrinkage \(\nu\) (update \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\nu\beta_mF_m\)), row subsampling (stochastic GB), tree size, leaf penalties (XGBoost). Small \(\nu\) with large \(M\) usually generalizes best.
  • Not variance-free. Boosting with strong base learners or noisy labels overfits. In the full-tree case below, the first tree already interpolates and the rest add nothing.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.15–18.16

  1. The model is a weighted sum of \(M\) base functions: \[ f(\mathbf x;\theta)=\sum_{m=1}^M\beta_mF_m(\mathbf x;\theta_m) \tag{18.15} \] Bagging/forests: \(\beta_m=\tfrac1M\) and each \(F_m\) is a tree fit on its own. If the \(\theta_m\) were fixed, this would be linear regression on the features \(F_m(\mathbf x)\). Here the features themselves are learned: "adaptive basis functions".
  2. The training objective is the usual empirical loss (plus an optional regularizer): \[ \mathcal L(f)=\sum_{i=1}^N\ell\big(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i)\big) \tag{18.16} \]
  3. Bagging's average has the same expectation as one member, \(\mathbb E\big[\tfrac1M\sum_mF_m(\mathbf x)\big]=\mathbb E[F_1(\mathbf x)]\), so its bias equals one tree's bias. Average 100 stumps fit to resamples of a sine-like curve and you still get something close to a single step. Only the jumpy variance is reduced.
  4. In boosting, \(F_m\) depends on \(F_1,\dots,F_{m-1}\). With stumps, after \(M\) rounds \(f\) is a step function with up to \(M\) jumps, so it can follow any shape in 1-D. Bias falls with \(M\). Variance rises slowly, so test error is U-shaped in \(M\); pick \(M\) by validation and stop early.
  5. Weak learner: training accuracy at least \(\tfrac12+\gamma\) on every reweighting the algorithm produces. Then the boosted training error falls exponentially in \(M\) (proved for AdaBoost in §18.5.3's grad box).

§18.5.1

Forward stagewise: add one term, freeze it, repeat

Optimizing all \(M\) terms of Eq. 18.15 at once is hard. Forward stagewise additive modeling keeps everything already in the model fixed and solves only for the next term. At iteration \(m\):

\[ (\beta_m,\theta_m)=\arg\min_{\beta,\theta}\sum_{i=1}^N\ell\big(y_i,\ f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big) \tag{18.17} \]

\[ f_m(\mathbf x)=f_{m-1}(\mathbf x)+\beta_mF_m(\mathbf x),\qquad F_m(\mathbf x)=F(\mathbf x;\theta_m) \tag{18.18} \]

The earlier parameters are never revisited. How to solve Eq. 18.17 depends on the loss \(\ell\) and on the weak learner \(F\). The next three sections work it out for squared, exponential and log loss.

Explain itAt your level

Build a tower one block at a time, and never pull out a block you already placed.

You're trying to make a tower match a wavy drawing. You get one block at a time. For each block, you pick where it goes (that's \(\theta\)) and how tall it is (that's \(\beta\)) so the tower looks as close to the drawing as it can right now. Then the block is glued down. Next block, same thing.

You never go back and move an old block. That makes each choice easy, even if the final tower isn't the very best one possible.

At each step the model is "what we have so far" plus "one new piece times a size". We search over the new piece's shape and size to make the total error as small as possible, then lock it in.

The error rule (the loss) matters. Squared error cares a lot about big misses, so one weird point can drag the new piece toward it. Absolute error cares less about big misses, so it shrugs off outliers. In the Play box, add outliers and switch the loss.

Eq. 18.17 is a small optimization over \((\beta,\theta)\) with \(f_{m-1}\) as a fixed offset. Here \(F\) is a sign stump, \(F(x;t)=-1\) if \(x\le t\) and \(+1\) otherwise, so \(\theta=t\). For each \(t\) we line-search \(\beta\), then keep the best \(t\).

Because \(F(x_i)=\pm1\), for any loss of the residual \(\ell(y,\hat y)=\rho(y-\hat y)\) with \(\rho\) symmetric: \(\rho(r_i-\beta F_i)=\rho(F_ir_i-\beta)\). So \(\beta\) is a location estimate of the "signed residuals" \(F_ir_i\): their mean for squared loss, median for absolute loss, and the Huber M-estimate for Huber loss.

Not adjusting earlier terms is the difference between stagewise and stepwise (which refits all coefficients each step). Stagewise fitting is slower to converge but acts as a regularizer.

  • Functional coordinate descent. Eq. 18.17 is exact line search along the "coordinate" \(F(\cdot;\theta)\) chosen from the dictionary of all weak learners. Gradient boosting (§18.5.6) replaces the exact inner argmin by fitting \(F\) to the negative gradient \(-\partial\ell/\partial f(\mathbf x_i)\), which is what makes arbitrary losses cheap.
  • Lasso connection. Stagewise with tiny steps (\(\epsilon\)-boosting) on a fixed dictionary traces approximately the lasso path (exactly, under monotonicity conditions; Efron et al. 2004, LARS).
  • Backfitting is the "go back" alternative: cycle through the terms, refitting each to the partial residuals of the others. It is the classic GAM fitter.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.17–18.18 with a sign stump

  1. Start with \(f_0(\mathbf x)=0\) (or a constant). At step \(m\) the residuals are \(r_i=y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\), and these are fixed numbers during the step.
  2. For a residual loss \(\ell(y,\hat y)=\rho(y-\hat y)\), the step objective is \[ \sum_i\rho\big(r_i-\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big). \] With \(F_i=\pm1\) and \(\rho\) symmetric, \(\rho(r_i-\beta F_i)=\rho\big(F_i(F_ir_i-\beta)\big)=\rho(F_ir_i-\beta)\).
  3. So for a fixed \(\theta\), the best \(\beta\) is a location estimate of \(s_i=F_ir_i\): \[ \begin{aligned}\beta^*_{\text{sq}}&=\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_is_i\\ \beta^*_{\text{abs}}&=\operatorname{median}(s_i)\\ \beta^*_{\text{Huber}}&=\arg\min_\beta\textstyle\sum_i\rho_\delta(s_i-\beta)\end{aligned} \] The mean is pulled by one huge residual; the median is not. That's why the dashed proposal chases outliers only under squared loss.
  4. Then pick \(\theta_m\) (here the threshold) with the smallest \(\min_\beta\) loss, and update \[ f_m(\mathbf x)=f_{m-1}(\mathbf x)+\beta_mF_m(\mathbf x). \tag{18.18} \] Nothing about \(F_1,\dots,F_{m-1}\) or \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_{m-1}\) changes. The loss can only go down (\(\beta=0\) is always allowed).

§18.5.2

Least squares boosting: fit the next tree to the residuals

With squared error \(\ell(y,\hat y)=(y-\hat y)^2\), the \(i\)'th term of Eq. 18.17 becomes

\[ \begin{aligned}\ell\big(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big)&=\big(y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)-\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big)^2\\&=\big(r_{im}-\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big)^2\end{aligned} \tag{18.19} \]

where \(r_{im}=y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\) is the residual. A regression tree has free leaf values, so \(\beta\) can be absorbed into them: set \(\beta=1\) and fit \(F\) to the residuals by ordinary least squares. This is least squares boosting [BY03]. Fig. 18.6 shows it with depth-2 regression trees.

Explain itAt your level

Guess, measure how far off you are, then make a second guess about the leftover.

Guess the heights of your classmates. Your first guess is rough. Now write down, for each kid, how much you missed by. Your next helper doesn't guess the heights; it guesses those misses. Add the two guesses together, and you're closer. Then a third helper guesses what's still missing, and so on.

A residual is "actual minus predicted". After the first tree, plot the residuals: they're the part of the pattern the first tree missed. Fit a second small tree to those points, add its prediction to the first, and the leftover shrinks.

In the Play box, the left plot shows the residuals and the new tree \(F_m\). The right plot shows the running total \(f_m\). Step through and watch the residuals flatten out toward pure noise.

Minimizing \(\sum_i(r_{im}-\beta F(\mathbf x_i))^2\) over a regression tree \(F\): for any fixed partition, the optimal \(\beta w_j\) is the mean residual in leaf \(j\). So \(\beta\) is redundant (\(\beta=1\)), and the step is "grow a CART regression tree on \((\mathbf x_i,r_{im})\)".

The training MSE never increases. With depth-\(d\) trees, enough steps drive it to the noise floor and then below it (overfitting), so \(M\) is chosen on held-out data.

  • Gradient view. \(r_{im}=-\tfrac12\,\partial(y_i-f)^2/\partial f\big|_{f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)}\). Fitting the residual is fitting the negative gradient; for other losses that gives gradient boosting (§18.5.6).
  • Shrinkage. In practice \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\nu F_m\) with \(\nu\in[0.01,0.1]\). Friedman found it consistently helps test error, at the cost of more trees. Géron's version of Fig. 18.6 uses \(\nu=1\).
  • L2Boosting (Bühlmann & Yu 2003) with smoothing-spline learners: each step is a linear smoother \(S\), so \(\hat{\mathbf f}_m=(I-(I-S)^m)\mathbf y\). Bias decays exponentially and variance grows slowly, which gives an explicit bias-variance analysis of \(M\).

Math, slowlyEquation 18.19

  1. Put squared loss into Eq. 18.17 and group the known parts: \[ \big(y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)-\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big)^2=\big(r_{im}-\beta F(\mathbf x_i;\theta)\big)^2 \tag{18.19} \] with \(r_{im}=y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\). Step \(m\) is a least squares problem with targets \(r_{im}\) instead of \(y_i\).
  2. A regression tree is \(F(\mathbf x)=\sum_jw_j\mathbb I(\mathbf x\in R_j)\), so \(\beta F=\sum_j(\beta w_j)\mathbb I(\mathbf x\in R_j)\). The leaf values absorb \(\beta\): set \(\beta=1\).
  3. For a fixed partition, the best leaf value is the mean residual in the leaf: \[ w_j=\frac{\sum_ir_{im}\mathbb I(\mathbf x_i\in R_j)}{\sum_i\mathbb I(\mathbf x_i\in R_j)} \] Exactly Eq. 18.3 with \(y\) replaced by \(r\). The partition itself is grown greedily with the MSE cost (18.6).
  4. Worked step: before step 1, \(f_0=0\), so \(r_{i1}=y_i\) and \(F_1\) is a plain tree on \(y\). At step 2, a point with \(y_i=0.40\) in a leaf where \(F_1=0.31\) has \(r_{i2}=0.09\). \(F_2\) tries to explain these leftovers. If \(F_2\)'s leaf there predicts \(0.07\), then \(f_2(\mathbf x_i)=0.38\), closer to \(0.40\).
  5. Training error can't go up: \(\sum_i(r_{im}-F_m(\mathbf x_i))^2\le\sum_ir_{im}^2\), because \(F\equiv0\) is a feasible tree (and a root-only tree with mean residual already does at least as well).

§18.5.3

Exponential loss and AdaBoost: upweight the mistakes

For binary labels \(\tilde y\in\{-1,+1\}\), let the model output \(F(\mathbf x)\) be half the log odds, so \(p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)=1/(1+e^{-2F(\mathbf x)})\) (18.20). The negative log likelihood is \(\ell=\log(1+e^{-2\tilde yF(\mathbf x)})\) (18.21). Both depend only on the margin \(m(\mathbf x)=\tilde yF(\mathbf x)\): positive means correct, and bigger is more confident.

The exponential loss \(\ell(\tilde y,F(\mathbf x))=\exp(-\tilde yF(\mathbf x))\) (18.22) is another smooth upper bound on the 0-1 loss with the same population minimizer, and it makes Eq. 18.17 easy to solve. With binary weak learners \(F_m\in\{-1,+1\}\), the result is discrete AdaBoost: fit \(F_m\) to the data weighted by \(\omega_{i,m}=e^{-\tilde y_if_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)}\), set \(\beta_m=\tfrac12\log\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m}\), and multiply the weight of every misclassified example by \(e^{\alpha_m}\), \(\alpha_m=2\beta_m\).

Explain itAt your level

Every time the team misses an example, that example gets bigger, so the next player can't ignore it.

Picture the practice examples as dots. They all start the same size. A helper draws one straight line to split red from blue and gets some dots wrong. Those wrong dots puff up. The next helper wants to get the big dots right most of all, so they draw their line somewhere else.

Helpers who did well get a louder voice in the final vote, and helpers who were barely better than guessing get a quiet voice. Step through the Play box and watch the dots grow.

The margin is "label times score": positive if the model is right, negative if it's wrong, large if it's confident. Different losses punish margins differently. The 0-1 loss is a cliff (1 if wrong, 0 if right). The others are smooth slopes that sit above that cliff, so pushing them down also pushes errors down.

Exponential loss grows very fast for negative margins: an example the model gets badly wrong costs a lot. AdaBoost turns that cost into a weight on each example. After each round, weights of the mistakes are multiplied by \(e^{\alpha}\), and the next stump trains on the reweighted data.

The stump's vote \(\alpha=\log\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}}\) is big when its weighted error is small, and 0 at err = ½ (a coin flip gets no say).

Write \(L_m(F)=\sum_i\omega_{i,m}e^{-\beta\tilde y_iF(\mathbf x_i)}\) with \(\omega_{i,m}=e^{-\tilde y_if_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)}\). Split the sum into correct and wrong cases: it equals \((e^\beta-e^{-\beta})\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\mathbb I(\tilde y_i\ne F(\mathbf x_i))+e^{-\beta}\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\). For any \(\beta\gt0\) the best \(F\) minimizes weighted training error (18.29). Setting \(\partial L_m/\partial\beta=0\) gives \(\beta_m=\tfrac12\log\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m}\) (18.30).

The new weights are \(\omega_{i,m+1}=\omega_{i,m}e^{-\beta_m\tilde y_iF_m(\mathbf x_i)}\). Using \(-\tilde yF=2\mathbb I(\tilde y\ne F)-1\) and dropping the common factor \(e^{-\beta_m}\): multiply mistakes by \(e^{\alpha_m}\), leave the rest (18.35).

Population minimizer (18.23–25): \(\mathbb E[e^{-\tilde yF}\mid\mathbf x]\) is smallest at \(F^*=\tfrac12\log\frac{p(\tilde y=1|\mathbf x)}{p(\tilde y=-1|\mathbf x)}\), the same as log loss. Check it with the slider below.

  • Training error bound. With normalized weights, \(Z_m=\sum_i\bar\omega_{i,m}e^{-\beta_m\tilde y_iF_m}=2\sqrt{\epsilon_m(1-\epsilon_m)}\), and \(\frac1N\sum_i\mathbb I(\tilde y_if_M(\mathbf x_i)\le0)\le\frac1N\sum_ie^{-\tilde y_if_M(\mathbf x_i)}=\prod_mZ_m\). The Play box draws this bound.
  • After the update, \(F_m\) has weighted error exactly ½. The reweighting makes the last learner useless on the new weights, which forces the next one to be different.
  • Margins. Test error often keeps falling after training error hits 0. Schapire et al. (1998) explain this with the distribution of normalized margins \(\tilde yf/\sum|\alpha_m|\), which keeps growing.
  • Multiclass. SAMME [Has+09] uses \(\alpha_m=\log\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m}+\log(K-1)\), so a learner only needs to beat \(1/K\). This is scikit-learn's AdaBoostClassifier.
  • Real AdaBoost lets \(F_m\) output \(\tfrac12\log\frac{p_w(y=1|\mathbf x)}{p_w(y=-1|\mathbf x)}\) from a weighted class-probability estimate.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.20–18.35

Why exponential loss is a reasonable choice

  1. If \(F\) is half the log odds, the class probability is \[ p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{F(\mathbf x)}}{e^{-F(\mathbf x)}+e^{F(\mathbf x)}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2F(\mathbf x)}} \tag{18.20} \] and the negative log likelihood is \(\ell(\tilde y,F)=\log(1+e^{-2\tilde yF})\) (18.21).
  2. Expected exponential loss at a fixed \(\mathbf x\), and its derivative: \[ \begin{aligned}\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\mathbb E\big[e^{-\tilde yF}\mid\mathbf x\big]&=\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\Big[p(\tilde y=1|\mathbf x)e^{-F}+p(\tilde y=-1|\mathbf x)e^{F}\Big]\\&=-p(\tilde y=1|\mathbf x)e^{-F}+p(\tilde y=-1|\mathbf x)e^{F}\end{aligned} \tag{18.23–24} \]
  3. Set it to zero: \[ \frac{p(\tilde y=1\mid\mathbf x)}{p(\tilde y=-1\mid\mathbf x)}=e^{2F(\mathbf x)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F^*=\tfrac12\log\frac{p}{1-p}. \tag{18.25} \] The same half-log-odds that log loss targets. With infinite data both losses aim at the same function; they differ in how they treat finite samples and outliers.

Solving step m (discrete AdaBoost)

  1. Pull the old model out of the exponent: \[ \begin{aligned}L_m(F)&=\sum_{i=1}^N\exp\!\big[-\tilde y_i(f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+\beta F(\mathbf x_i))\big]\\&=\sum_{i=1}^N\omega_{i,m}\exp(-\beta\tilde y_iF(\mathbf x_i))\end{aligned} \tag{18.26} \] \(\omega_{i,m}=e^{-\tilde y_if_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)}\) is large exactly for points the current model gets wrong (negative margin).
  2. With \(F\in\{-1,+1\}\), \(\tilde yF=+1\) when correct and \(-1\) when wrong: \[ \begin{aligned}L_m&=e^{-\beta}\!\!\sum_{\tilde y_i=F(\mathbf x_i)}\!\!\omega_{i,m}+e^{\beta}\!\!\sum_{\tilde y_i\ne F(\mathbf x_i)}\!\!\omega_{i,m}\\&=(e^\beta-e^{-\beta})\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\mathbb I(\tilde y_i\ne F(\mathbf x_i))+e^{-\beta}\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\end{aligned} \tag{18.27–28} \]
  3. For \(\beta\gt0\), \(e^\beta-e^{-\beta}\gt0\), so the best weak learner minimizes weighted error: \[ F_m=\arg\min_F\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\mathbb I(\tilde y_i\ne F(\mathbf x_i)) \tag{18.29} \]
  4. Divide by \(\sum_i\omega_{i,m}\) and write \(\mathrm{err}_m\) for the weighted error (18.31). Minimize \(e^{-\beta}(1-\mathrm{err}_m)+e^{\beta}\mathrm{err}_m\): the derivative is zero when \(e^{2\beta}=\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m}\), \[ \beta_m=\frac12\log\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m} \tag{18.30} \] Example: err = 0.2 gives \(\beta=\tfrac12\log4=0.693\), \(\alpha=2\beta=1.386\), and mistakes get multiplied by \(e^{1.386}=4\). err = 0.5 gives \(\beta=0\).
  5. Update the model, \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\beta_mF_m\) (18.32), and the weights: \[ \begin{aligned}\omega_{i,m+1}&=e^{-\tilde y_if_m(\mathbf x_i)}=\omega_{i,m}e^{-\tilde y_i\beta_mF_m(\mathbf x_i)}\\&=\omega_{i,m}e^{2\beta_m\mathbb I(\tilde y_i\ne F_m(\mathbf x_i))}e^{-\beta_m}\end{aligned} \tag{18.33–34} \] Uses \(-\tilde y_iF_m(\mathbf x_i)=2\mathbb I(\tilde y_i\ne F_m(\mathbf x_i))-1\).
  6. \(e^{-\beta_m}\) is the same for every \(i\) and cancels when weights are normalized. With \(\alpha_m=2\beta_m\): \[ \omega_{i,m+1}=\begin{cases}\omega_{i,m}e^{\alpha_m}&\tilde y_i\ne F_m(\mathbf x_i)\\ \omega_{i,m}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} \tag{18.35} \] and the classifier is \(\operatorname{sgn}\big[\sum_m\alpha_mF_m(\mathbf x)\big]\). Scaling all \(\beta\) by 2 doesn't change the sign.

§18.5.4

LogitBoost: log loss, bounded influence, real probabilities

Exponential loss has two problems. It blows up for negative margins, so it puts a lot of weight on misclassified examples, and mislabeled ones (outliers) can pull the fit around. And \(e^{-\tilde yf}\) is not the log of any pmf, so \(f\) is not trained as a probability model.

Log loss fixes both: it grows only linearly for large negative margins, and probabilities come straight out of the fitted function:

\[ p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{f(\mathbf x)}}{e^{-f(\mathbf x)}+e^{f(\mathbf x)}}=\frac1{1+e^{-2f(\mathbf x)}} \tag{18.36} \]

The stage-\(m\) objective is \(L_m(F)=\sum_i\log\big[1+\exp\big(-2\tilde y_i(f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+F(\mathbf x_i))\big)\big]\) (18.37). One Newton step on it (as in IRLS) turns each round into a weighted least squares fit. That's LogitBoost [FHT00].

Explain itAt your level

What if some answers in the answer key are just wrong?

AdaBoost keeps puffing up every example it misses. If the answer key has a typo, the team can never get that example "right," so it keeps puffing up and up until it's huge and the team spends all its effort on a mistake.

LogitBoost is calmer. It still cares more about missed examples, but there's a limit on how much any one example can matter. A typo can't take over the whole practice.

Look at Fig. 18.7 again: on the left, the exponential loss shoots up like a rocket, while log loss climbs along a straight line. The slope of the loss is how hard the next round pulls toward that example. Exponential: the pull grows without limit. Log loss: the pull tops out.

Bonus: with log loss, \(f\) is half the log odds, so \(1/(1+e^{-2f})\) is a real probability you can trust, like "73% spam." In the Play box, flip some training labels and compare the two methods.

Influence of example \(i\) = \(|\partial\ell/\partial f(\mathbf x_i)|\). Exp loss: \(e^{-m_i}\), unbounded as the margin \(m_i\to-\infty\). Log loss \(\log(1+e^{-2m})\): \(2/(1+e^{2m_i})\le2\). A mislabeled point sits at negative margin forever, so AdaBoost keeps raising its weight.

LogitBoost round (Algorithm 18.2): with \(y^*_i=(\tilde y_i+1)/2\in\{0,1\}\) and \(p_i\) from 18.36, the Newton step for 18.37 is a weighted regression of working responses \(z_i=\frac{y^*_i-p_i}{p_i(1-p_i)}\) with weights \(w_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). Fit \(F_m\) by weighted least squares, update \(f\leftarrow f+\tfrac12F_m\), recompute \(p_i\).

The key subroutine is a weak learner that solves weighted least squares, e.g. a regression tree with sample weights.

  • Newton derivation. Let \(g(F)=\sum_i\ell_i(f_{m-1}+F)\) with \(\ell_i\) the Bernoulli NLL in \(2f\). The pointwise gradient and Hessian are \(-2(y^*_i-p_i)\) and \(4p_i(1-p_i)\). The Newton step \(F=-g'/g''\) is \(\tfrac12z_i\), and projecting onto the weak-learner class with Hessian weights gives the weighted LS fit (the IRLS step of logistic regression, §10.2.6).
  • Numerics. \(z_i\) explodes as \(p_i\to0\) or \(1\). FHT00 cap \(|z_i|\le z_{\max}\in[2,4]\); this page uses 4. Modern GBDT (XGBoost) uses the same gradient/Hessian per leaf: leaf value \(-\sum g_i/(\sum h_i+\lambda)\).
  • Multiclass: symmetric multinomial LogitBoost fits \(K\) functions with \(\sum_kf_k=0\).
  • Robustness has limits. Any convex margin loss can be broken by random label noise (Long & Servedio 2010); bounded influence helps a lot in practice but isn't a guarantee.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.36–18.37 and Algorithm 18.2

  1. Influence = slope of the loss in \(f\), at margin \(m=\tilde yf\): \[ \Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial f}e^{-\tilde yf}\Big|=e^{-m}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial f}\log(1+e^{-2\tilde yf})\Big|=\frac{2}{1+e^{2m}}\le2. \] At \(m=-3\): \(e^{3}\approx20\) for exp loss, \(2/(1+e^{-6})\approx2\) for log loss. A flipped label stuck at margin −3 gets 10× more pull under AdaBoost.
  2. Probabilities from the score (18.36): \(p(y=1|\mathbf x)=1/(1+e^{-2f(\mathbf x)})\). With log loss this is the model being fit, so it is calibrated in the large-sample limit.
  3. Stage-\(m\) objective (18.37): \(L_m(F)=\sum_i\log\big[1+\exp\big(-2\tilde y_i(f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+F(\mathbf x_i))\big)\big]\). Newton's method around \(F=0\): with \(y_i^*=\frac{\tilde y_i+1}2\), \(p_i=\sigma(2f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i))\), \[ \frac{\partial L_m}{\partial F_i}\Big|_0=-2(y_i^*-p_i),\qquad\frac{\partial^2L_m}{\partial F_i^2}\Big|_0=4p_i(1-p_i). \]
  4. A second-order expansion is a weighted least squares problem: \[ L_m(F)\approx\text{const}+2\sum_ip_i(1-p_i)\Big(F(\mathbf x_i)-\tfrac12z_i\Big)^2,\qquad z_i=\frac{y^*_i-p_i}{p_i(1-p_i)}. \] Fit \(F_m\) to targets \(z_i\) with weights \(w_i=p_i(1-p_i)\), then add half of it (the \(\tfrac12\) in \(\tfrac12z_i\)).
Algorithm 18.2: LogitBoost (binary, [FHT00])
1\(w_i=1/N,\ p_i=1/2,\ f(\mathbf x)=0\)
2for \(m=1:M\) do
3  Compute working response \(z_i=\dfrac{y_i^*-p_i}{p_i(1-p_i)}\)
4  Compute weights \(w_i=p_i(1-p_i)\)
5  \(F_m=\arg\min_F\sum_iw_i\,(z_i-F(\mathbf x_i))^2\)
6  Update \(f(\mathbf x)\leftarrow f(\mathbf x)+\tfrac12F_m(\mathbf x)\)
7  Compute \(p_i=1/(1+\exp(-2f(\mathbf x_i)))\)
8Return \(f(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{sgn}\big[\sum_{m=1}^MF_m(\mathbf x)\big]\)
The weight \(p_i(1-p_i)\) is small for a confidently misclassified point, but its working response \(z_i\approx\pm1/\min(p_i,1-p_i)\) is huge. Their product \(w_iz_i=y^*_i-p_i\) is (half) the gradient, and it never exceeds 1 in size: that is the bounded influence. In practice \(z_i\) is also capped (here at ±4) to keep the least squares fit numerically stable.
